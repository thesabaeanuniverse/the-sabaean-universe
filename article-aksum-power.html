Aksum: A Red Sea Power in Late Antiquity

Aksum grew into a major state of the northern Horn whose political center lay in the highlands while its trade and influence reached the Red Sea and beyond.

Evidence
Primary sources, archaeology, documented objects.
Interpretation

A highland kingdom with maritime reach

UNESCO describes the Aksumite kingdom as a major first-millennium state situated at the crossroads of Africa, Arabia, and the Greco-Roman world. Its wealth is visible in monumental stelae, royal tombs, palace remains, inscriptions, and a long-lived urban center.

The political geography changed through time, so any single map of Aksum should be read as a dated reconstruction rather than an eternal border. What is clear is that the highland capital and the Red Sea port of Adulis belonged to the same wider political and commercial world during Aksum’s strongest centuries.

Monuments and political display

The stelae fields at Aksum contain some of the most technically ambitious monolithic monuments of the ancient world. Their scale, carved architectural details, and association with elite tombs make them evidence of organized labor, stone-working expertise, and concentrated political resources.

Interpretation: monumental scale demonstrates power and resources, but it does not by itself reveal the full structure of government or the lived experience of ordinary people.

Inscriptions as royal voice

Stone inscriptions attributed to Aksumite rulers are among the most important sources for political history. UNESCO notes that some inscriptions of King Ezana survive in Greek, Sabaean script, and Geʿez, giving historians an unusually multilingual record of royal communication.

Royal texts must still be read critically. They describe authority from the ruler’s point of view and often emphasize victory, legitimacy, piety, and conquest.
