The Great Temple at Yeha: Architecture, Cult, and Evidence

The Great Temple at Yeha is one of the strongest archaeological anchors for studying Almaqah in the northern Horn because monument, dedication, and regional context converge at a single major site.

Evidence
Inscriptions, archaeology, documented objects.
Interpretation
Scholarly readings and historical models.
Reconstruction
Project-level synthesis kept visibly separate.

A seventh-century BCE monument

DAI research dates the surviving Great Temple at Yeha to the middle of the seventh century BCE. Its exterior walls survive to roughly fourteen meters in height, making the building an unusually well-preserved sacred structure from the first millennium BCE.

The masonry consists of carefully dressed limestone blocks set with exceptional precision. DAI notes that the building follows architectural schemes known from ancient South Arabia, while also belonging to an archaeological landscape in the northern Horn with its own long occupation history.

Dedicated to Almaqah

Archaeological publications and UNESCO documentation identify the temple as dedicated to Almaqah. That association belongs in the category of evidence-based historical description.

Scholarly attribution: some archaeological publications describe Almaqah as a Sabaean moon deity. This website treats that celestial identification as an attributed scholarly interpretation unless a specific inscription directly establishes the association under discussion.

What the building can and cannot prove

The temple demonstrates organized labor, architectural expertise, cultic investment, and strong cultural links across the Red Sea. It cannot, by itself, determine the full ethnic identity of its users, the political status of every community in the region, or the total theology of Almaqah.

Method: monument = evidence of architecture and cultic context; broader claims about identity and cosmology require additional inscriptional or comparative support.
