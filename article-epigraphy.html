Reading the Stones: An Evidence-First Epigraphy Method

The archive begins with the photograph, not with a reconstructed sentence. That simple rule helps prevent decorative or modern imitation script from becoming confused with ancient evidence.

Step 1: Preserve the source image

Keep the original photograph unchanged in the archive whenever possible. Cropped, resized, sharpened, or color-corrected versions may be useful for presentation, but the original source image should remain available for direct comparison.

This creates a clear chain between the archaeological object and every later reading, transliteration, translation, or reconstruction.

Step 2: Separate visible reading from restoration

Visible characters, damaged characters, restored readings, transliteration, and translation should not be collapsed into a single line without explanation.

Each stage introduces a different level of interpretation. A damaged sign that has been restored by an editor is not equivalent to a sign clearly visible on the object.

Evidence rule: what can be seen on the object should be distinguished from what has been restored, normalized, supplied, translated, or historically interpreted.

Step 3: Record provenance

Each inscription entry should eventually include as much provenance as can be established: findspot, archaeological context, present location if known, publication reference, object type, material, approximate date, script, language, dimensions when available, and bibliographic history.

Provenance matters because the same text can mean something very different when found on a temple dedication, altar, funerary monument, royal inscription, reused stone, or object removed from its original context.

Step 4: Label uncertainty

If a reading is disputed, the website should show competing readings or mark the uncertain portion rather than forcing a single confident answer.

A broken sign, unclear word division, uncertain personal name, or damaged title should remain visibly uncertain until stronger evidence resolves it.

Step 5: Distinguish transcription, transliteration, and translation

These are related but different tasks.

Transcription records the written signs as faithfully as possible. Transliteration represents those signs using a defined scholarly notation. Translation expresses the interpreted meaning in another language.

Keeping these stages separate makes disagreements easier to identify. Two scholars may agree on the visible letters but disagree on word division, grammatical analysis, or translation.

Step 6: Separate attestation from interpretation

An inscription can directly attest a name, title, divine name, place, dedication, verb, genealogy, or political formula. Historical conclusions built from those statements belong to a second analytical level.

The publication therefore uses the same three-part method throughout: Evidence, Interpretation, and Reconstruction.

Visual rule across all projects:

1. Authentic supplied inscription image.
2. Verified published inscription image.
3. If neither is available, use no inscription image.

Generated imitation script is not used as a substitute for an ancient inscription.

Why this matters

Epigraphy is strongest when the reader can move backward from a historical claim to the translation, from the translation to the transliteration, and from the transliteration back to the photographed object.

That chain makes the evidence inspectable. It also makes mistakes easier to find and corrections easier to document.
