Horizon Astronomy: How Landscapes Can Become Calendars

Repeated observation of sunrise and sunset positions against a stable horizon can turn mountains, ridges, towers, or other landmarks into practical seasonal reference points.

Research boundary: The article explains a testable observational method. It does not assign an astronomical function to any Horn monument without archaeological evidence.
Evidence
Textual, astronomical, archaeological, or observational data.
Interpretation
Historical explanation based on those data.
Reconstruction
HCM-364 or other project-level models kept separate.

The principle is simple

The Sun does not rise at exactly the same point on the horizon every day. Across the year, its rising and setting positions migrate between seasonal extremes and then reverse direction.

When observers repeatedly compare those positions with stable landmarks, the landscape can function as a seasonal measuring frame.

What archaeoastronomy requires

Modern archaeoastronomy combines astronomical calculation with archaeology, geography, statistics, history, and anthropology. Orientation alone is not enough: site chronology, viewing location, cultural context, horizon profile, and alternative explanations matter.

The well-studied site of Chankillo in Peru is a useful methodological comparison because its thirteen towers span the annual solar rising and setting arcs when viewed from specific observation points.

Applying the method to the Horn

For a Senafe or northern-Horn observation to become historical evidence, the project would need repeatable measurements, dated archaeological context, a defined observing point, horizon coordinates, and some independent reason to connect the landscape with timekeeping.

Observation is not ancient attribution: demonstrating that a modern observer can track the Sun against a ridge does not prove ancient people used that exact ridge as a calendar marker. Archaeological or textual support is needed.

Sources and further reading
