Horizon Astronomy: How Ancient Landscapes Can Track the Sun

The Sun does not rise and set at exactly the same points throughout the year. Repeated observation of those changing positions against a stable horizon can turn mountains, ridges, towers, notches, and other landmarks into practical seasonal reference points.

Project landscape image used to illustrate the observational method. It is not presented here as proof that this specific location functioned as an ancient astronomical observatory.

Research boundary: demonstrating that the Sun aligns with a landscape feature today does not prove that ancient people intentionally used that feature for calendrical observation. Historical attribution requires archaeological, chronological, statistical, or textual support.
[A] Observation
Measured sunrise and sunset positions, horizon altitude, azimuth, solar declination, dates, photographs, and repeated field observations.
[B] Interpretation
Arguments that a particular horizon feature, monument, or viewing position may have been intentionally related to celestial observation.
[C] Reconstruction
Larger proposed calendrical or HCM-364 systems reconstructed from several lines of evidence.

The principle is simple

From a fixed location, the Sun's rising point shifts along the eastern horizon over the course of the year.

Its setting point moves correspondingly along the western horizon.

The movement is gradual and reversible. The Sun reaches one seasonal extreme, changes direction, crosses the middle part of its annual path, reaches the opposite extreme, and returns.

A stable horizon can therefore serve as a natural measuring scale.

+
stable horizon
+

The horizon is not just background

In observational astronomy, a distant ridge or mountain can function like a reference line.

A notch, summit, cliff edge, tower, standing stone, or architectural feature can provide a repeatable marker against which celestial motion is observed.

The usefulness of the marker depends on having a defined viewing position.

Move the observer, and the apparent relationship between the Sun and the horizon feature changes.

The observation point matters

One of the most common weaknesses in archaeoastronomical claims is failure to define exactly where the ancient observer would have stood.

A serious investigation should identify:

• the observation point

• its coordinates and elevation

• the direction being observed

• the horizon feature

• the horizon altitude

• the relevant celestial event

• the archaeological relationship between observer and marker

Azimuth

Horizon positions are commonly measured using azimuth.

Azimuth records direction around the horizon, usually beginning at north:

North = 0°

East = 90°

South = 180°

West = 270°

Sunrise azimuth changes through the year because the Sun's declination changes.

Horizon altitude

A mountainous horizon is not equivalent to a flat sea horizon.

If a ridge rises several degrees above the astronomical horizon, the Sun must climb higher before it becomes visible.

Therefore, a proper archaeoastronomical calculation must account for both horizontal direction and vertical horizon altitude.

Measurement rule: azimuth without horizon altitude is incomplete when the skyline is uneven.

Solar declination

Solar declination describes the Sun's angular position north or south of the celestial equator.

Over the annual cycle it moves between northern and southern extremes.

That changing declination is what produces the seasonal migration of sunrise and sunset positions.

The solstices

Near the solstices, the Sun reaches the extreme ends of its annual horizon range.

For several days around the turning point, the daily change in rising or setting position becomes relatively small.

This apparent pause is one reason solstitial positions can provide useful horizon markers.

The equinoxes

Around the equinoxes, the Sun crosses the celestial equator.

Sunrise occurs approximately toward the east and sunset approximately toward the west, although the exact observed position can be affected by horizon altitude, atmospheric refraction, and local geography.

The equinox should therefore not be identified merely by pointing toward an approximate compass direction.

Latitude matters

The apparent annual solar range depends on the observer's latitude.

A horizon system developed at one latitude cannot simply be transferred unchanged to another.

This matters when comparing astronomical traditions from the northern Horn, Mesopotamia, the Mediterranean, or the Americas.

Atmospheric refraction

Near the horizon, Earth's atmosphere bends incoming light.

This makes the Sun appear slightly higher than its purely geometric position.

Modern archaeoastronomical calculations therefore consider atmospheric refraction when precision matters.

The Sun has a visible disc

Sunrise is not a single mathematical instant unless the observer defines which part of the solar disc is being measured.

Possible definitions include:

first appearance of the upper limb

center of the Sun

full disc above the horizon

A repeatable field method should use the same definition each time.

Seasonal timekeeping without a clock

Horizon observation does not require a mechanical clock.

An observer returning to the same position can notice whether sunrise is moving toward or away from a particular ridge, notch, tower, or mountain.

Over repeated years, those positions can provide practical indicators of seasonal progress.

Landscape markers can become mnemonic devices

A memorable natural feature can make seasonal knowledge easier to transmit.

Instead of remembering an abstract angle, a community might remember a sunrise in relation to a specific summit or gap.

That possibility is anthropologically plausible, but each historical case still requires evidence.

Natural marker or constructed marker?

Horizon astronomy can use either.

A mountain is naturally present. A tower, wall, stela, doorway, or other monument is constructed.

Constructed markers may provide stronger evidence of intentional design, but only when their orientation, chronology, context, and astronomical relationship can be demonstrated.

Orientation alone is not enough

Almost every structure points somewhere.

If enough possible celestial targets are considered, some apparent alignments will occur by chance.

This is why archaeoastronomy requires more than measuring an angle and finding a nearby astronomical event.

An alignment becomes stronger when:

• the observation point is archaeologically meaningful

• the structure is securely dated

• the horizon target is clearly defined

• the astronomical event is appropriate to that date and latitude

• similar orientations recur in a meaningful pattern

• textual, ritual, iconographic, or ethnographic evidence supports the interpretation

• alternative explanations have been tested

Test alternative explanations

A wall may face a direction because of terrain, drainage, wind, access, building tradition, neighboring architecture, defense, or construction convenience.

A solar explanation is stronger only when it performs better than these alternatives.

Good archaeoastronomy therefore tries to disprove an alignment as well as prove it.

Chankillo as a methodological comparison

Chankillo in Peru is a useful comparison because its interpretation is not based on one isolated alignment.

A sequence of thirteen towers forms a horizon feature whose relationship with solar rising and setting positions can be evaluated from specific observation points.

Archaeological context, architecture, horizon geometry, and solar movement can therefore be studied together.

Comparative rule: Chankillo demonstrates a method for testing horizon astronomy. It does not prove that sites in the ancient Horn used the same system.

Why thirteen towers do not prove thirteen HCM Houses

Numerical resemblance is interesting, but it is not historical identity.

Chankillo's thirteen towers belong to their own archaeological and cultural context.

HCM-364's thirteen Celestial Houses belong to a modern reconstructed framework.

The shared number thirteen may inspire a comparative question, but it does not establish transmission or common origin.

Applying the method to the northern Horn

The highlands of Eritrea and northern Ethiopia contain complex natural horizons: mountain peaks, escarpments, ridges, passes, volcanic formations, and long-distance sightlines.

These landscapes make horizon astronomy a legitimate subject for field investigation.

The research question is not whether the Sun can be observed against these horizons. It obviously can.

The historical question is whether particular ancient communities intentionally incorporated such observations into timekeeping, architecture, ritual, agriculture, or political practice.

A proper field record

A repeatable observation program should record the same categories each time.

Recommended observation record:

• site name

• observation-point coordinates

• elevation

• date

• local time

• direction of observation

• horizon azimuth

• horizon altitude

• celestial body

• first-visible or center-disc definition

• weather and atmospheric conditions

• photograph from fixed position

• archaeological notes

• confidence level

Repeat observations

One photograph is rarely sufficient.

Repeated observations across days, seasons, and ideally multiple years help distinguish a real pattern from coincidence, atmospheric effects, camera placement, or memory.

A fixed camera position or accurately surveyed observation point makes the record much stronger.

Modern calculation can test ancient possibilities

Astronomy software can calculate where the Sun, Moon, Venus, and stars would have appeared for a particular location and date.

For ancient periods, calculations can also account for long-term celestial changes such as precession where relevant.

But software should test archaeological hypotheses rather than manufacture them.

The date must come from archaeology

An astronomical alignment calculated for 700 BCE is useful only if the monument or observation context can plausibly be dated to that period.

Otherwise the researcher risks choosing whichever historical date gives the most attractive alignment.

Chronology rule: archaeology should constrain the astronomical calculation, not the other way around.

Textual evidence can strengthen the case

An inscription mentioning the Sun, a season, a month, a festival, a rising, a gate, or another astronomical concept can materially strengthen an interpretation when securely associated with the site.

The wording still has to be read in its original linguistic and cultural context.

A general religious reference to a celestial body does not automatically establish a precise architectural alignment.

Ritual timing is another line of evidence

If independent evidence shows that ceremonies occurred at a particular seasonal point, a matching solar alignment may become historically more meaningful.

Again, the strength comes from convergence:

archaeology
+
orientation
+
astronomy
+
chronology
+
cultural evidence

Observation is not ancient attribution

This distinction is central to THE SABAEAN UNIVERSE research method.

A modern observer may successfully use a mountain ridge to track the annual Sun.

That proves the landscape is capable of functioning as a solar reference.

It does not by itself prove that ancient inhabitants used the same feature in the same way.

Where HCM-364 enters

HCM-364 can use horizon observations as a way to test parts of its reconstructed celestial framework.

For example, a proposed House boundary or seasonal transition might be compared with measured solar positions.

But the reconstruction should make predictions before the observations are interpreted.

Better test:

HCM predicts a position or date
↓
field observation tests it

Weaker test:

observe many alignments first
↓
choose the ones that resemble HCM afterward

A falsifiable model is stronger

A reconstruction becomes more useful when it can fail.

If HCM-364 predicts a repeatable horizon relationship and accurate observation does not find it, the model should be revised.

That approach prevents the system from absorbing every possible result.

The four evidence levels should not collapse

Horizon astronomy involves several distinct steps:

Observed:
the Sun rose at a measured position.

Calculated:
astronomical geometry explains that position.

Historically interpreted:
an ancient community may have intentionally observed it.

Reconstructed:
the observation may belong to a larger proposed calendrical system.

These statements have different evidentiary strength.

What would count as strong evidence in the Horn?

A convincing ancient-Horn case would ideally combine several independent forms of evidence.

• securely dated archaeological context

• repeatable observation point

• clearly measured horizon target

• statistically meaningful orientation

• appropriate ancient celestial event

• supporting inscription, ritual, iconography, or calendar evidence

• evidence that alternative practical explanations are weaker

The strongest conclusion

Horizon astronomy is a real and testable observational method.

The annual movement of sunrise and sunset can make a stable landscape function as a practical seasonal reference frame.

Archaeological interpretation requires a higher standard.

A visible alignment becomes evidence of ancient intentionality only when astronomy converges with archaeological and cultural context.

[A] Attested / observed

The Sun's horizon position changes systematically through the year and can be measured against stable landmarks.

[B] Defensible comparative

Archaeological sites such as Chankillo demonstrate that some ancient societies intentionally built or used horizon systems for solar observation.

[C] HCM reconstruction

Any claim that a particular northern-Horn landmark, monument, House, or Gate formed part of HCM-364 remains a testable reconstruction until independent archaeological evidence supports it.
