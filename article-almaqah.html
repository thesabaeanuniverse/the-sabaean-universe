Almaqah in the Northern Horn: What Is Attested?

Almaqah is important because the divine name appears within the same broader archaeological world that preserves Sabaean script, monumental architecture, and cultic connections across the Red Sea.

Start with the inscriptional record

The strongest method begins with objects and inscriptions that can be placed in archaeological context. Where a divine name is actually present in an inscription, that fact should be separated from later attempts to reconstruct the deity’s complete theology, celestial identity, or symbolic system.

At Yeha, the Great Temple is associated with Almaqah, and research on Yeha and related Ethio-Sabaean sites has examined cultic, architectural, and epigraphic links with Saba.

Attestation is not a complete theology

An inscription naming a deity proves that the name was used in that context. A temple dedication can establish cultic importance. Neither automatically tells us everything worshippers believed about that deity.

Project standard: claims about Almaqah’s symbolism, astronomical associations, or ritual meaning should be graded by evidence. Directly attested names and dedications belong in Evidence. Broader symbolic models belong in Interpretation or Reconstruction unless supported by explicit sources.

Why this matters for HCM-364

HCM-364 can compare later or reconstructed celestial frameworks with ancient religious evidence, but it should not retroactively rewrite inscriptions. The strongest approach is to preserve the original evidence intact, then ask whether a reconstruction explains patterns without pretending the reconstruction is itself ancient text.
