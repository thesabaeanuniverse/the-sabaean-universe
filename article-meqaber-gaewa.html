Meqaber Gaʿewa: A Temple, a Royal Dedication, and a Political Network

The Almaqah sanctuary at Meqaber Gaʿewa near Wukro gives unusually specific evidence because archaeology, cult objects, and a royal inscription survive together.

Evidence
Inscriptions, archaeology, documented objects.
Interpretation
Scholarly readings and historical models.
Reconstruction
Project-level synthesis kept visibly separate.

A temple of the early first millennium BCE

DAI reports that the sanctuary was constructed during the eighth to sixth centuries BCE, with research describing a single-room temple and three-part sanctuary. The site preserves cult objects, architectural material, and inscriptions of major historical value.

The building is smaller and structurally different from the Great Temple at Yeha. UNESCO notes that its walls used irregular stone and clay mortar, while Yeha’s temple used finely dressed ashlar masonry.

The royal dedication of Waʿran

DAI reports an Ethio-Sabaean votive inscription on a limestone altar naming King Waʿran. According to the institute’s reading, the text refers to the king building the temple when the principal sanctuary of Almaqah at Yeha was entrusted to him.

This is important because it links ruler, sanctuary, Yeha, and Almaqah in a single inscriptional context.

Local and Sabaean traditions together

The same archaeological assemblage complicates any simple one-directional cultural model. DAI points to features it connects with northeast African traditions, including the mention of the king’s mother, a seated female votive statue, and pottery that fits the wider cultural context of the northern Horn.

Evidence pattern: the site contains strong Sabaean-linked forms and clearly local archaeological material in the same cultural setting.
