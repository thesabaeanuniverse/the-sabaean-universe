Adulis: The Port Connecting Aksum to the Red Sea

Adulis, on the Red Sea coast of present-day Eritrea, was a major maritime settlement associated with the Aksumite world and an important node connecting the northern Horn with Red Sea, Mediterranean, Arabian, and Indian Ocean exchange.

[A] Evidence
Archaeological layers, architecture, ceramics, imported materials, ancient written sources, and securely documented objects.

A port with a long history

Archaeological and literary evidence places Adulis firmly within the Red Sea commercial world during the early centuries CE, while excavated material indicates a longer settlement history extending further back into the first millennium BCE.

Adulis became one of the most important coastal settlements associated with the Aksumite world. Its location connected inland routes from the highlands with maritime networks running through the Red Sea and into the western Indian Ocean.

Imported ceramics, transport containers, building materials, and other finds demonstrate connections extending beyond the immediate region.

Chronology rule: Adulis had a long history. Evidence from one period should not automatically be projected onto every other phase of the settlement.

The highlands and the sea

Aksum's principal political center lay inland, but participation in long-distance maritime exchange depended on access to the Red Sea coast. Adulis provided an important point where seaborne traffic could connect with routes leading toward the northern Horn highlands.

This relationship is important because Aksumite history cannot be reconstructed from the highland capital alone. Coastal archaeology provides another part of the same wider historical system.

Evidence boundary: association between Adulis and the Aksumite world is historically important, but the exact political status and degree of royal control over the port must be assessed for specific periods rather than assumed to have remained unchanged for centuries.

What moved through Adulis?

Ivory is repeatedly associated with the ancient Red Sea commerce of the region, but Adulis participated in a much wider movement of commodities, people, technologies, languages, and ideas.

Imported containers and other archaeological materials provide physical evidence for connections with distant ports. Products originating in the Horn also entered exchange networks extending toward Arabia, the Mediterranean, and the Indian Ocean.

Trade is more than imported objects

Imported artifacts establish contact. They do not automatically tell us who transported the goods, who financed the exchange, who controlled the port, or how commodities were distributed after reaching the coast.

Archaeologists therefore combine imported materials with settlement patterns, architecture, chronology, local ceramics, written sources, environmental evidence, and regional transport routes.

This separates the direct evidence for long-distance contact from broader historical interpretations about economic organization and political authority.

Religious change at the port

Archaeological work at Adulis has contributed to the study of Christianity and religious transition in the Late Antique Horn. Excavated church structures and their construction phases provide material evidence for Christian activity at the settlement.

This evidence is useful because religious change can be studied through architecture, stratigraphy, chronology, and spatial organization rather than through later literary traditions alone.

Method: archaeological evidence for Christian buildings establishes the presence and material expression of Christianity in particular contexts. Broader conclusions about the beliefs of every inhabitant require additional evidence.

A cosmopolitan setting

Ports often bring together people, goods, languages, technologies, and religious traditions from different regions. Adulis therefore provides a different window onto the Aksumite world from inland royal monuments, inscriptions, and elite funerary architecture.

Coastal material can reveal connections that are less visible in inland political records.

Political status through time

The relationship between Adulis and Aksum should be treated chronologically. A port can participate in the commercial sphere of a kingdom without maintaining exactly the same administrative status through every period.

Evidence for royal authority, trade, religion, architecture, and population therefore needs to be dated independently before being combined into one historical reconstruction.

What can be said securely

Adulis was an important settlement on the Red Sea coast of present-day Eritrea and participated extensively in long-distance maritime exchange. During the Aksumite period it formed part of the wider political and commercial world connected with the highlands.

Archaeology documents substantial settlement activity, imported materials, long-distance contact, and Late Antique Christian architecture.

More specific claims about political administration, harbor organization, population composition, or territorial control require evidence tied to defined chronological periods.

Evidence → Interpretation → Reconstruction

Evidence: archaeological layers, ceramics, architecture, imported objects, ancient texts, and dated religious structures.

Interpretation: arguments about commerce, political authority, religious transition, and connections with Aksum.
