From Pre-Aksumite to Aksumite: Break or Continuity?

The transition from the early first-millennium BCE political landscape to Aksum was once easier to describe as a sharp break. Archaeological evidence now makes that picture more complicated.

Beta Samati changes the discussion

Excavations at Beta Samati documented occupation beginning in the Pre-Aksumite period and continuing through the Aksumite era. The excavators argue that this evidence challenges older models in which the Yeha region was largely abandoned after the Pre-Aksumite period.

The importance of this evidence is not that it proves every institution survived unchanged. Instead, it demonstrates that communities, settlement, and regional activity could persist across periods that were once treated as sharply separated.

Continuity does not mean sameness

Aksum developed political, economic, and cultural forms that differed in significant ways from earlier first-millennium BCE societies. Kingship, monumental display, long-distance trade, coinage, written royal ideology, and eventually Christian institutions became important features of the Aksumite world.

Settlement continuity therefore should not be confused with institutional identity. A community can remain in the same region while its political organization, religious practices, trade relationships, technologies, and social structures change substantially.

Interpretation: continuity is best treated as a pattern to be tested through individual sites and specific categories of evidence, not as proof that DʿMT and Aksum were identical political systems.

A better historical question

Instead of asking whether one civilization simply disappeared and another suddenly replaced it, archaeology allows more precise questions.

Which settlements remained occupied? Which monuments were reused? Which technologies continued? Which religious traditions changed? Which political centers gained or lost importance? How did Red Sea trade routes shift? Which forms of writing persisted or changed?

These questions can be tested separately and can produce different answers. Continuity in settlement, for example, does not automatically demonstrate continuity in political administration.

The problem with rigid historical periods

Labels such as “Pre-Aksumite” and “Aksumite” are useful for organizing chronology, but archaeological reality does not always change at the same moment that modern period labels change.

Material traditions may overlap. Settlements can continue while political centers shift. Older religious or architectural practices may survive alongside new institutions.

For that reason, the transition should be reconstructed from dated archaeological contexts rather than assumed from the names of historical periods alone.

Yeha within the longer sequence

Yeha remains important because it preserves evidence from an early monumental and literate landscape with strong Sabaean connections. Nearby evidence from Beta Samati extends the regional history across a much longer chronological span.

Together, these sites support a model in which the northern Horn underwent substantial transformation while also preserving important forms of local continuity.

What can be said securely

Archaeology supports long-term settlement in parts of the northern Horn across the Pre-Aksumite and Aksumite periods. It also demonstrates major changes in political scale, economic networks, monumental traditions, and religious institutions.

The evidence therefore does not require a choice between total continuity and total replacement. Different forms of continuity and change occurred together.

Evidence → Interpretation

Evidence: dated occupation levels, architecture, inscriptions, ceramics, artifacts, settlement patterns, and other archaeological remains.

Interpretation: historical models explaining how those changes relate to political identity, cultural continuity, migration, and the emergence of Aksum.
