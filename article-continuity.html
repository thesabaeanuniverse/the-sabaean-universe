From Pre-Aksumite to Aksumite: Break or Continuity?

The transition from the early first-millennium BCE political landscape toward the Aksumite period was once easier to describe as a sharp break. Archaeological evidence now makes that picture more complicated.

Beta Samati changes the discussion

Excavations at Beta Samati documented occupation beginning in the Pre-Aksumite period and continuing through the Aksumite era. The excavators argue that this evidence challenges older models in which the Yeha region was largely abandoned after the Pre-Aksumite period.

The importance of this evidence is not that it proves every institution survived unchanged. Instead, it demonstrates that communities, settlement, and regional activity could persist across periods that were once treated as sharply separated.

Evidence rule: continuous occupation at a site is evidence for settlement continuity. It is not, by itself, proof that political institutions, religious systems, languages, identities, or ruling structures remained unchanged.

Continuity does not mean sameness

Aksum developed political, economic, and cultural forms that differed in significant ways from earlier first-millennium BCE societies. Kingship, monumental display, long-distance exchange, coinage, written royal ideology, and eventually Christian institutions became important features of the Aksumite world.

Settlement continuity therefore should not be confused with institutional identity. A community can remain in the same region while political organization, religious practices, trade relationships, technologies, language use, and social structures change substantially.

Interpretation: continuity is best treated as a pattern to be tested through individual sites and specific categories of evidence, not as proof that DʿMT and Aksum were identical political systems.

Ask more precise questions

Instead of asking whether one civilization simply disappeared and another suddenly replaced it, archaeology allows narrower and more testable questions.

Settlement
Which sites remained occupied, contracted, expanded, or were abandoned?
Material culture
Which ceramic, architectural, technological, and craft traditions continued or changed?
Institutions
Which forms of political authority, religious practice, writing, trade, and monumental display changed over time?

These questions can produce different answers. Continuity in settlement, for example, does not automatically demonstrate continuity in political administration.

The problem with rigid historical periods

Labels such as “Pre-Aksumite” and “Aksumite” are useful tools for organizing chronology, but archaeological reality does not necessarily change at the exact moment that a modern period label changes.

Material traditions may overlap. Settlements can continue while political centers shift. Older religious or architectural practices may survive alongside new institutions. Writing practices can persist while languages, scripts, political titles, or administrative uses change.

For that reason, transitions should be reconstructed from dated archaeological contexts rather than assumed from period names alone.

Yeha within the longer sequence

Yeha remains important because it preserves evidence from an early monumental and literate landscape with strong Sabaean connections. Nearby evidence from Beta Samati extends regional occupation across a much longer chronological span.

Together, these sites contribute to a picture in which the northern Horn underwent substantial transformation while also preserving important forms of local continuity.

Continuity can operate at different levels

Population

Communities

People may remain in a region across major political and cultural transformations.

Landscape

Places

Settlements, roads, agricultural zones, monuments, and ritual places can persist or be reused.

Institutions

Systems

Political structures, religious institutions, economic networks, and writing traditions may change even when settlement continues.

Change can also occur at different speeds

Political power may reorganize quickly while pottery styles change slowly. Religious institutions can transform while some architectural techniques persist. Long-distance trade routes may expand while local settlement patterns remain comparatively stable.

Archaeology therefore rarely requires an all-or-nothing choice between “continuity” and “replacement.” Different components of a society can change at different rates.

What can be said securely

Archaeology supports long-term occupation in parts of the northern Horn across the Pre-Aksumite and Aksumite periods. It also documents major changes in political scale, economic networks, monumental traditions, written expression, and religious institutions.

The evidence therefore does not require a choice between total continuity and total replacement. Different forms of continuity and transformation occurred together.

Evidence → Interpretation

Evidence: dated occupation levels, architecture, inscriptions, ceramics, objects, settlement patterns, burials, coins, imported goods, and other archaeological remains.

Interpretation: historical models explaining how these changes relate to political identity, population history, cultural continuity, migration, exchange, and the emergence of Aksum.

Why this matters for THE SABAEAN UNIVERSE

The project should not begin with the assumption that every early northern Horn tradition either vanished completely or continued unchanged.

Instead, each proposed continuity must identify the specific evidence being compared: language with language, architecture with architecture, inscriptions with inscriptions, religious practice with religious evidence, astronomy with independently documented astronomical practice.

Research standard: similarity across two periods is a reason to investigate continuity. It is not, by itself, proof of uninterrupted institutional identity.

Continuity and HCM-364

The same standard applies to HCM-364. If the reconstructed system proposes that an astronomical, calendrical, linguistic, or symbolic structure persisted across long periods, that continuity must be tested rather than assumed.

Strong evidence would require securely dated examples showing that the same relevant structure, terminology, observation method, or institutional practice persists across the proposed chronological sequence.
