Visual Epigraphy: Reading Ancient Inscriptions from Images

Ancient inscriptions should be studied from authentic source images whenever possible. Digital presentation can improve visibility, but it must never replace damaged or missing ancient writing with invented text.

Authentic project-archive inscription photograph used here as an example of visual epigraphy. The image is not presented as an Almaqah dedication unless independent provenance establishes that association.

Evidence
Original photographs, visible letter forms, object surfaces, damage, material, scale, provenance, and archaeological context.
Interpretation
Character identification, restoration, transliteration, translation, dating, and historical reading.
Reconstruction
Proposed missing passages, reconstructed layouts, or historical models that extend beyond what remains physically visible.

The source image comes first

A high-quality photograph is not merely decoration. It is part of the evidentiary record.

Readers should be able to compare a transcription or transliteration with the actual marks preserved on the stone, altar, stela, architectural block, or portable object.

For that reason, THE SABAEAN UNIVERSE treats the original photograph as the starting point of the epigraphic workflow.

Permanent visual rule:

authentic supplied inscription → verified published inscription → no inscription.

Generated imitation is not a substitute for ancient evidence.

Preserve the archival master

Whenever possible, the highest-quality unaltered version of an inscription photograph should be retained as the archival master.

Edited versions may then be created for publication, web display, diagrams, teaching panels, or social media.

This preserves a clear distinction between the original documentation and later presentation work.

What image editing can do

Responsible image processing can make inscriptions easier to inspect.

Useful presentation adjustments may include cropping, rotation, perspective correction, exposure adjustment, contrast enhancement, sharpening, and tonal correction.

These procedures can reveal surface details that are already present in the photograph.

What image editing must not do

Editing must not silently create ancient letters that are not visible in the source.

It must not convert uncertain traces into complete characters simply because a particular reading would fit the expected word.

It must not replace damaged sections with aesthetically convincing pseudo-Musnad or other invented writing.

Core distinction: enhancing visibility is presentation. Creating missing letter forms is reconstruction.

Damage is evidence too

Cracks, erosion, missing corners, worn surfaces, recutting, ancient breaks, and later damage are not visual defects to be erased automatically.

They can help explain why an inscription is difficult to read and may contain information about the history of the object.

If part of an inscription is unreadable, the photograph should remain unreadable there unless a separately labeled reconstruction is provided.

Do not redraw uncertain characters into certainty

Ancient letter forms can vary by period, region, stone surface, carving technique, and individual hand.

An unclear mark may resemble more than one sign.

A responsible transcription records that uncertainty rather than forcing the image to match a preferred interpretation.

Photography and transcription are different evidence layers

A photograph records the visible object.

A transcription represents a scholar's reading of those visible marks.

These are related but they are not identical.

Recommended sequence:

Photograph
↓
Visible-character assessment
↓
Diplomatic transcription
↓
Restoration, clearly marked
↓
Transliteration
↓

Diplomatic transcription

A diplomatic transcription aims to represent the preserved text as closely as practical.

Missing signs, uncertain characters, damaged passages, line divisions, separators, and unusual spellings should remain visible in the scholarly presentation.

Normalization belongs to a later layer.

Restoration must be visible

Restoring missing text is a legitimate part of epigraphy when supported by surviving traces, grammar, formulaic parallels, or closely related texts.

But reconstructed text must be distinguishable from preserved text.

A reader should never have to guess which letters are physically present and which letters have been supplied by an editor.

Transliteration is not the inscription itself

Transliteration converts ancient signs into a modern scholarly notation.

It allows comparison, searching, linguistic analysis, and publication, but it remains a representation of the ancient writing rather than the original object.

THE SABAEAN UNIVERSE should therefore display the authentic inscription image alongside transliteration whenever the source is available.

Translation adds another interpretive layer

Translation requires decisions about vocabulary, grammar, titles, religious terminology, and historical context.

A translation should not be placed back onto an image in a way that makes modern English appear to be part of the ancient artifact.

Image, transliteration, and translation should remain visually distinct.

Provenance changes the value of an image

A clear photograph without provenance can still preserve useful letter forms, but its historical value is more limited.

A photograph tied to an excavated object, documented findspot, museum inventory, publication, material description, and archaeological layer has much greater interpretive power.

Visual epigraphy therefore requires both image quality and source history.

Minimum archive information

Each inscription record should eventually store more than a photograph.

Recommended inscription record:

• Record ID
• Original image
• Site / findspot
• Present location
• Museum or inventory number, if available
• Material
• Dimensions
• Script
• Language
• Dating and dating basis
• Diplomatic transcription
• Restoration notes
• Transliteration
• Translation
• Bibliography
• Confidence notes
• Related ruler, sanctuary, monument, or site

Why generated pseudo-script is dangerous

AI-generated ancient-looking writing can be visually convincing while containing malformed characters, mixed scripts, impossible sequences, or completely invented signs.

A casual reader may not recognize the difference.

Once such imagery is published beside historical discussion, it can be copied elsewhere and gradually mistaken for genuine evidence.

This is why generated pseudo-Musnad has no place in an evidentiary panel.

Illustration is different from evidence

A modern artistic reconstruction can still be useful if it is clearly labeled as an illustration.

The problem is not reconstruction itself. The problem is presenting modern reconstruction with the visual authority of a primary source.

Labeling rule:

If the writing is ancient, identify the source.
If the writing is restored, mark the restoration.
If the image is reconstructed, label it reconstruction.
If the ancient text is unavailable, do not manufacture a substitute.

Authentic Musnad presentation

When verified Sabaean Musnad text is available from a published source or authentic inscription, it may be displayed alongside transliteration and translation.

The exact ancient reading should be preserved as closely as the publication and Unicode representation allow.

Where a character cannot be verified confidently, uncertainty should be stated rather than silently replaced with an assumed sign.

Image comparison can reveal useful details

Multiple photographs of the same inscription may reveal details that are difficult to see in one image.

Side lighting, viewing angle, resolution, shadow, and weathering can all affect apparent letter shapes.

Comparing independent photographs can therefore improve reading without altering the artifact.

Photogrammetry and 3D documentation

Where available, photogrammetry and three-dimensional surface models can improve the study of shallow or damaged inscriptions.

Such methods can preserve surface geometry and allow researchers to inspect carved depth and lighting from multiple virtual angles.

Digital models remain documentation tools. Their interpretation must still be distinguished from the physical marks they record.

Visual evidence and Almaqah research

For Almaqah-related material, authentic inscription photographs are particularly important because claims may involve divine names, royal titles, dedicatory formulas, sanctuary relationships, and religious terminology.

A modern decorative representation of the divine name cannot carry the same evidentiary weight as an authenticated inscription.

The source object must remain primary.

Visual evidence and HCM-364

HCM-364 may use inscriptional evidence as part of a broader comparative reconstruction.

But the matrix must never alter inscription photographs to make ancient material resemble the reconstructed system.

If an inscription supports a comparison, that support should come from the authentic text or archaeological context—not from a redesigned image.

[A] Attested
Authentic inscription image and securely documented archaeological information.

[B] Defensible comparative
Comparison among verified inscriptions, forms, sites, or documented objects.

[C] Reconstruction
Restored passages, proposed historical relationships, visual diagrams, or HCM-364 models extending beyond direct attestation.

A future inscription database

The long-term technical value of this archive will come from linking each inscription image to structured data rather than storing photographs as disconnected media files.

A searchable inscription database could connect rulers, divine names, sites, monuments, scripts, languages, dates, bibliographies, and related archaeological objects.

That would allow the project to move from a visual archive toward a reproducible research system.

The principle is simple

Ancient inscriptions already carry uncertainty because of erosion, breakage, incomplete excavation, and difficult language.

Digital publication should reduce confusion, not add another layer of invented material.

Preserve the photograph. Mark restoration. Cite provenance. Separate translation from transcription. Label reconstruction.

When no authentic inscription is available, leaving the space empty is more accurate than filling it with a convincing false one.

Evidence → Interpretation → Reconstruction

Evidence: original image, visible marks, object surface, material, dimensions, provenance, archaeological context, and documented publication history.

Interpretation: character identification, transcription, transliteration, translation, dating, and historical analysis.

Reconstruction: restored passages, reconstructed layouts, proposed symbolic meanings, and larger models not physically preserved on the ancient object.
