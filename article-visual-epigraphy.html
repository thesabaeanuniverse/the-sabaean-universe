Visual Epigraphy: Reading Ancient Inscriptions from Images

Ancient inscriptions should be studied from source images whenever possible. Digital presentation can improve visibility, but it must never replace damaged or missing ancient writing with invented text.

Project archive inscription photograph used as a visual-epigraphy example. It is not presented as an Almaqah dedication or assigned to a specific inscription corpus here unless independent provenance establishes that identification.

[A] Attested
Source photographs, visible characters, physical surfaces, material, damage, scale, provenance, archaeological context, and documented publication history.
[B] Interpretation
Character identification, dating, transliteration, translation, linguistic analysis, and historical reading.
[C] Reconstruction
Restored passages, proposed missing characters, reconstructed layouts, or historical models extending beyond what remains physically visible.

The source image comes first

A useful inscription photograph is not merely decoration. It is part of the evidentiary record.

Readers should be able to compare a proposed transcription or transliteration with the marks preserved on the stone, altar, stela, architectural block, or portable object.

THE SABAEAN UNIVERSE therefore treats the source photograph as the first visual layer in the epigraphic workflow.

Permanent visual rule

authentic user-supplied or securely documented inscription

Generated pseudo-script is not a substitute for ancient evidence.

Preserve the archival master

Whenever possible, the highest-quality unaltered version of an inscription photograph should be retained as the archival master.

Edited derivatives may then be created for web publication, diagrams, teaching material, books, or social media.

This preserves a traceable distinction between original documentation and later presentation work.

Archive principle: never overwrite the only available source image with an enhanced, annotated, cropped, color-graded, or reconstructed version.

What image editing can do

Responsible image processing can make surviving details easier to inspect.

Useful adjustments can include cropping, rotation, perspective correction, exposure adjustment, tonal correction, contrast enhancement, and restrained sharpening.

These procedures should reveal information already captured by the photograph rather than manufacture information that was never recorded.

What image editing must not do

Editing must not silently create ancient letters that are absent from the source image.

It must not convert uncertain traces into complete characters merely because one reading produces an expected word.

It must not replace damaged sections with aesthetically convincing pseudo-Musnad or other invented writing.

Damage is evidence too

Cracks, erosion, missing corners, worn surfaces, recutting, ancient breaks, weathering, and later damage are not merely visual defects.

They can explain why a reading is uncertain and may preserve information about the history of the object itself.

If an area of an inscription cannot be read, the source photograph should remain unreadable there. Any proposed reconstruction belongs in a separate, explicitly marked layer.

Do not redraw uncertainty into certainty

Ancient letter forms vary according to period, region, material, carving technique, available space, and individual hand.

A damaged mark may plausibly represent more than one sign.

A responsible edition records that uncertainty rather than altering the image until it resembles the preferred reading.

Epigraphic rule: uncertainty in the stone should remain uncertainty in the edition until stronger evidence resolves it.

Photograph and transcription are different layers

The photograph records the visible object.

A transcription records a researcher's reading of the visible signs.

The two layers are connected, but they are not identical.

Diplomatic transcription

A diplomatic transcription aims to represent the preserved writing as faithfully as practical.

Missing areas, uncertain signs, damaged passages, line divisions, word separators, and unusual spellings should remain visible in the scholarly presentation.

Normalization belongs to a later analytical layer.

Restoration must remain visible

Restoring missing text is a legitimate part of epigraphy when supported by surviving traces, grammatical structure, available space, formulaic parallels, or related inscriptions.

A strong restoration can still be a reconstruction.

Readers should never have to guess which characters physically survive and which have been supplied by an editor.

Preserved
Character or trace physically visible on the ancient object.
Uncertain
Surviving trace permits more than one plausible reading.
Restored
Character or passage supplied through scholarly reconstruction.

Transliteration is not the ancient object

Transliteration represents ancient signs using a modern scholarly notation.

It enables linguistic analysis, searching, indexing, comparison, and publication, but remains a representation of the ancient writing rather than the inscription itself.

A declared transliteration standard should therefore be applied consistently.

Normalization is a separate operation

Linguistic normalization can be useful for grammar, indexing, dictionary comparison, or reconstruction of a conventional form.

It should never silently replace the spelling preserved by the ancient inscription.

Translation adds interpretation

Translation requires decisions about grammar, vocabulary, royal titles, divine terminology, damaged phrases, and historical context.

Where more than one translation is defensible, the alternatives should be stated rather than hidden behind one apparently certain English rendering.

Translation should remain visually separate from the source image so that modern wording is never mistaken for writing physically present on the ancient artifact.

Provenance changes evidentiary value

A clear photograph without provenance may still preserve useful letter forms.

Its historical usefulness becomes much greater when the image can be tied to a documented object, findspot, museum inventory, excavation record, material description, chronology, and publication.

Visual epigraphy therefore requires both image quality and source history.

Why generated pseudo-script is dangerous

Computer-generated ancient-looking writing can appear convincing while containing malformed signs, mixed alphabets, impossible combinations, or completely invented characters.

A reader unfamiliar with the script may not recognize the difference.

Once such an image circulates beside historical content, it can be copied elsewhere and gradually misidentified as genuine evidence.

Project prohibition: pseudo-Musnad or invented ancient writing must never appear in an evidentiary panel as though it were an inscription.

Illustration is different from evidence

A modern reconstruction or artistic diagram may still be useful when its purpose is clearly stated.

The problem is not reconstruction itself. The problem is allowing modern reconstruction to acquire the visual authority of a primary source.

If the writing is ancient → cite the source.

If characters are restored → mark the restoration.

If an image is reconstructed → label it reconstruction.

If no authentic inscription is available → do not manufacture one.

Verified Musnad presentation

When verified Sabaean Musnad text is available from an ancient inscription or reliable publication, it can be shown alongside transliteration and translation.

The ancient character sequence should be preserved as closely as the source and Unicode representation permit.

Where a character cannot be verified confidently, uncertainty is preferable to silently inserting an assumed sign.

Multiple photographs can improve a reading

Different photographs of the same inscription may reveal details that are difficult to see in one image.

Side lighting, viewing angle, resolution, surface moisture, shadows, and weathering can change the apparent form of shallow carved lines.

Comparing independent photographs can therefore improve epigraphic analysis without modifying the object.

Photogrammetry and 3D documentation

Where available, photogrammetry and three-dimensional surface models can be useful for shallow, weathered, or damaged inscriptions.

They can preserve surface geometry and permit inspection under different virtual lighting directions.

Digital models remain documentation tools. The interpretation of a carved feature remains distinct from the geometry recorded by the model.

Visual evidence and Almaqah research

For Almaqah-related material, inscription photographs matter because claims may involve divine names, rulers, titles, dedicatory formulas, sanctuaries, and religious terminology.

A decorative modern rendering of the divine name cannot carry the same evidentiary weight as a documented ancient inscription.

The source object remains primary.

Visual evidence and HCM-364

HCM-364 may compare inscriptional evidence with a wider reconstructed framework.

The matrix must never alter inscription photographs or editorial readings so ancient evidence appears to contain the expected HCM structure.

If an inscription supports a comparison, that support must arise from the authentic source and its historical context.

Direction of analysis: source object → transcription → translation → historical interpretation → HCM comparison. Never HCM expectation → modified source object.
Evidence → Interpretation → Reconstruction

Evidence: source image, visible marks, object surface, material, dimensions, provenance, archaeological context, and documented publication history.

Interpretation: character identification, transliteration, translation, dating, linguistic analysis, and historical interpretation.

Reconstruction: restored passages, missing signs, reconstructed layouts, proposed symbolic meanings, or wider historical and HCM-364 models not physically preserved on the ancient object.

A future inscription database

The long-term research value of the archive will increase when each inscription image is connected to structured metadata instead of remaining an isolated media file.

A searchable database could connect rulers, divine names, places, sanctuaries, monuments, scripts, languages, dates, bibliography, related artifacts, and confidence levels.

Such a system would allow visual comparison while preserving the distinction between source, reading, interpretation, and reconstruction.

The principle is simple

Ancient inscriptions already contain uncertainty because of erosion, breakage, incomplete excavation, damaged surfaces, and difficult language.

Digital publication should reduce that uncertainty where possible, not add another layer of invented material.

Preserve the source image.

Preserve provenance.

Mark uncertainty.

Mark restoration.

Separate transliteration from translation.

Label reconstruction.

When the ancient evidence is missing, leave it missing.
